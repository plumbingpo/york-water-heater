Here is what shortens most water heaters' lives: corrosion from the inside and mineral buildup on the bottom, both of which are largely preventable. As water heats, dissolved minerals come out of the water and settle into a layer of sediment on the tank floor. On a gas unit, that layer insulates the water from the burner, so the steel runs hotter and the tank starts to rumble and pop. On an electric unit, it builds up around the lower element until the element burns out. Meanwhile the anode rod, a metal rod designed to corrode so the tank does not, dissolves a little every month. Once it is gone, the tank itself becomes the target. In York, PA, we see this pattern constantly, and most of it was preventable.

The work is hands-on and specific. We drain the tank and flush out sediment until the water runs clean. We pull the anode rod to see how much protection remains and replace it when needed. We lift the T&P valve lever to confirm it opens, then watch it reseat without dripping. For gas equipment, we inspect the burner and flame, pilot or ignition parts, thermocouple or flame sensor, and the vent and draft. For electric equipment, we meter the elements and check the thermostats and wiring. Then we check the expansion tank's pressure, confirm a safe temperature setting near 120 degrees, and look over every fitting for early signs of a leak.

Tankless owners need maintenance too, just a different kind. Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, especially in hard water, and eventually triggers error codes, reduced flow, or temperature swings. We flush the heat exchanger with a pump and descaling solution through the service valves, clean the inlet filter, check the venting and condensate line on condensing models, and review the unit's error history. When to Call for Water Heater Maintenance

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • A tank that pops and knocks during a heating cycle
  • A water heater that has never been flushed or inspected
  • Less hot water available than when the tank was new
  • Rusty or discolored hot water at the tap
  • Temperature swings or reduced flow from a tankless heater
  • Moisture, drips, or rust around the base or fittings
  • A smell like rotten eggs that shows up only on the hot side
  • Higher gas or electric costs for the same routine

Why Professional Water Heater Maintenance Matters

A few maintenance tasks suit homeowners, and many do not. Keeping clutter away from the heater, checking for leaks, and noticing new noises are all useful. Flushing, anode replacement, relief valve testing, and burner or element work are a different matter. Hot water, seized fittings, brittle drain valves, and sediment that clogs the valve midway through can turn a simple job into a flooded floor. Relief valves on older heaters sometimes fail to close after testing. And gas-side checks involve flame quality and venting, which protect your household's air.

We come ready for the common surprises: a drain valve that will not reseal, an anode rod that has turned to wire, a relief valve that drips after testing. We carry replacements, along with meters for element and thermostat testing and a descaling kit for tankless units. More than parts, we bring a trained eye. Small signs, like rust at a connection, a weak flame, or a waterlogged expansion tank, point to future problems, and catching them early is the entire point of the visit. That is how a routine visit adds years to a heater's life.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Descale tankless units once a year, or more often in hard water
  • Keep at least a couple of feet clear around the heater for air and service access
  • Look around the base of the heater periodically for moisture
  • Consider a water softener if hard water keeps building sediment and scale
  • Keep the thermostat near 120 degrees to limit scalding and slow sediment buildup
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years and replaced when it is mostly gone
  • Flush annually, more often where the water is hard
